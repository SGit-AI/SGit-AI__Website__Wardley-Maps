Thirty-five concepts, classified

Every distinct Wardley-related idea in the corpus this site draws on, with the words it was first stated in, the date, and — the column that matters — whether it is standard doctrine, an extension, or an original contribution.

A correction to our own source. The research table this page is generated from opens by declaring "33 entries — 14 [O], 13 [E], 6 [S]". Parsing it yields 35 rows classified 17 original / 14 extension / 4 standard. Four rows are lettered sub-entries (W6b, W13b, W15b, W25b) of a parent concept, which explains the row count but not the class split. The parse is what this page and the endpoint publish, because the table is the artefact and the header is a summary of it. Logged as N2.

That classification is the site's editorial spine, and it is also the licence-safe move. Standard doctrine is Simon Wardley's: his book says it first and better, and restating CC BY-SA material as though it were ours would both mislead and infect the page's licence. So standard doctrine is linked, never rewritten. Extensions and originals are what this site is for.

Two vocabulary problems, fixed here

Spelling. The corpus uses commoditise / commoditize and productise / productize interchangeably, sometimes in the same document. This site uses -ise throughout, to match the rest of the network. The -ize spellings are the same word and carry no different meaning; quotations keep whatever spelling they were written with.

Names. Explorer and Pioneer are the same thing; so are Villager and Settler. The rename is stated outright exactly once in the entire corpus — parenthetically, in a brief about something else. This site states it on every first use and never drifts: Explorer = Pioneer, Villager = Settler, Town Planner = Town Planner, after Simon Wardley, On Pioneers, Settlers, Town Planners and Theft (2015), CC BY-SA 3.0. The word Settler appears once in the entire corpus; it is not adopted here.

A teaching order, six rungs

The corpus is dense at the top and thin at the bottom: one 846-word primer, then a cliff. This is the ladder — and /start/ walks it.

1

What a map is

Anchor, position, evolution, the four stages. This belongs to Wardley — link out to the book and to Wardley Mapping 101; this site does not rewrite it.

2

How to read one

Three named reading techniques, each of which changes what you see.

User-needs-first doctrine (his statement of it) · **Paired before/after maps as the unit of argument** · **The commodity illusion**

3

How to write one that survives review

The coordinate contract, the parse rules, and the two rules that make a map arguable rather than decorative.

Wardley-maps-as-Mermaid-code · The node-naming parse-error caveat (empirical) · **A map is a falsifiable claim · Honest mapping / do not overclaim

4

Where doctrine bends

The site's centre of gravity. Four opinionated pages.

**The custom-axis verdict · Configurable / alternative evolution axes (openness · Same idea restated as visibility / risk overlays · **De-commoditisation / the attrition shield** · **The air gap as manual labour; the ends-evolved-middle-manual shape**

5

Maps as organisation

Pioneers–Settlers–Town Planners, applied to agent teams and to artefacts rather than to people.

Explorer / Villager / Town Planner · The "theft" / handover · **The lifecycle of a skill: Explorer → Product → Town Planner → absorbed** · **Explorer-phase discipline: defer Town-Planner artifacts** · **The vibe-coded MVP is the best brief; the three-team shipping workflow** · **Somebody has to be the villagers · The same skill differs by Wardley character

6

Maps as data

The most architecturally ambitious layer and the least implemented. Labelled as design, not as shipped.

A map is a graph with positional metadata · Doctrine assessment as a standing role responsibility · Gameplay identification (ILC · Map the control flows and the cost flows together · Every division has its own map and its own maturity
